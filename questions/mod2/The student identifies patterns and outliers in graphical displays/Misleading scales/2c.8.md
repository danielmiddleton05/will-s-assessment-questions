Misleading scales

##### Explanation
When a y-axis starts at $81,000 instead of $0, the visual difference between bars is dramatically exaggerated. Store A ($85,000) and Store B ($82,000) differ by only $3,000—roughly a 3.6% difference—yet the truncated scale makes Store A's bar appear nearly three times taller than Store B's. This visual distortion would lead viewers to incorrectly conclude that Store A vastly outperforms Store B, potentially justifying the resource reallocation based on misleading visual evidence rather than the actual modest difference in performance.

##### Distractors
###### Option 2
Store A generates at least double the weekly revenue of Store B — The truncated y-axis makes Store A's bar appear dramatically taller, which could lead some viewers to estimate a ratio of 2:1 or greater. However, Store A ($85,000) outsells Store B ($82,000) by only $3,000—a ratio of roughly 1.04:1. While this is a plausible visual exaggeration caused by the misleading scale, viewers are most likely to form the broader qualitative impression that Store A substantially outperforms Store B (Option 1) rather than estimate a specific numerical ratio from the bar heights.

###### Option 3
Store B contributes less than a quarter of the combined weekly sales — The truncated y-axis severely distorts the visual proportions of the bars. Because the baseline starts at $81,000, Store A's bar is four units tall ($81k to $85k) while Store B's bar is only one unit tall ($81k to $82k). A viewer relying strictly on the visual mass of the bars might incorrectly estimate a 4-to-1 ratio, leading to the assumption that Store B accounts for only 20% (less than a quarter) of the total revenue. In reality, Store B contributes nearly 50% ($82,000 / $167,000) of the combined sales. While this is a highly plausible mathematical misinterpretation driven by the visual distortion, viewers are most likely to take away the simpler qualitative impression that Store A is substantially better (Option 1).

###### Option 4
Both stores are underperforming compared to industry benchmarks — This introduces information (industry benchmarks) that is not present in or suggested by the graph. The visual presentation provides no reference point for performance standards, so a viewer could not reasonably draw this conclusion from the graph itself. This targets students who bring in external assumptions not supported by the displayed data.
