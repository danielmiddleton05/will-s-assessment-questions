#### Internal Notes

##### Domain
Health

##### Competency
The student interprets real-world data using graphical displays and descriptive statistics.

##### Evidence Statement

##### Aligned Content
Discrete vs continuous

##### Explanation
Discrete variables can only take specific, countable values—typically whole numbers. The number of hospital visits is discrete because a patient can have 0, 1, 2, 3 visits, etc., but not 2.7 visits. Blood pressure, body temperature, and waiting time are continuous because they represent measurements that can take any value within a range, even if they are often rounded to the nearest whole number in practice.

##### Distractors

###### Option 1
Blood pressure readings — Continuous measurement

Blood pressure is a measurement of force and represents a continuous scale. Students who select this may not recognize that measurements on a continuous scale, even when typically recorded as whole integers in a clinical setting, are fundamentally different from counts.

###### Option 2
Body temperature — Continuous measurement

Temperature can take any value within a range (e.g., 98.6°F, 99.2°F). Students may confuse the fact that thermometers display specific increments with the underlying continuous nature of temperature.

###### Option 4
Time spent waiting in the lobby — Continuous measurement

Time is a continuous variable and can be measured to any degree of precision (seconds, milliseconds, etc.). This distractor catches students who don't recognize that duration measurements are continuous even when typically reported in whole minutes.

Peaks/modes

##### Explanation
Standard deviation measures how spread out data values are from the mean. Although both routers share the same average of 50 Mbps, their individual trial results differ dramatically in spread. Router A's values (48–52) cluster tightly around the mean, deviating by only 1–2 Mbps per trial. Router B's values (30–70) are scattered far from the mean, with individual deviations as large as 20 Mbps. This means Router B has a much larger standard deviation—and therefore greater variability—than Router A. A shared average does not imply shared consistency.

##### Distractors

###### Option 1
Router A shows greater variability because no two trial results are exactly the same — Confuses uniqueness of data points with spread. The fact that all five values in Router A are distinct does not mean they are widely spread. All of Router A's values fall within 4 Mbps of the mean, indicating very low variability. Meanwhile, Router B has a repeated value (45) yet its values range 40 Mbps from the mean, demonstrating much greater variability.

###### Option 3
Both routers show equal variability because they share the same average speed — Confuses the mean (a measure of center) with standard deviation (a measure of spread). Two datasets can have identical means yet very different levels of variability. The mean tells us nothing about how spread out the individual values are.

###### Option 4
Router B shows lower variability because its wide range of results balances around the average — Incorrectly reasons that values spreading symmetrically around the mean creates consistency. A wider spread of data points around the average actually produces a larger standard deviation, indicating more variability, not less—regardless of whether the values appear to balance out.
