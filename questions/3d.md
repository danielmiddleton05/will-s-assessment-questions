### Task: wgu_a3ds1_Justifying Paired Data Design_r7Tm3

#### Task Description

**Given:** A technology company measures each user's app loading time before and after installing a software update. An analyst chooses to use a paired comparison to evaluate whether the update improved performance.

What feature of this study design justifies the use of a paired comparison rather than an independent samples comparison?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
Each loading time is recorded using the same measurement scale.


###### Option 2 Incorrect
The before and after groups contain equal numbers of observations.


###### Option 3 Correct
Each user provides measurements under both conditions.


###### Option 4 Incorrect
The measurements are collected at two separate points in time.

##### Competency
The student analyzes relationships between variables in real-world contexts, including correlation and regression.

##### Evidence Statement

##### Aligned Content
Paired vs unpaired data

##### Domain
Technology

##### Explanation
The data are paired because each individual user is measured twice—once before and once after the update—creating a natural link between the two observations. This linkage is the defining feature that distinguishes paired data from independent samples. The analyst can then examine within-user differences, which controls for variability between individuals.

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
Same measurement scale — Confuses measurement consistency with data structure

Students may believe that using the same units or scale is what allows measurements to be compared as pairs. While consistent measurement is good practice, it applies equally to independent samples designs and does not determine whether data are paired or unpaired.

###### Option 2
Equal group sizes — Confuses a consequence of pairing with its cause

Because each person is measured twice, paired data always produces equal counts in both conditions. Students who notice this pattern may mistakenly conclude that equal sample sizes are what make data paired, reversing cause and effect. Two independent groups could also happen to have equal sizes without being paired.

###### Option 4
Two time points — Confuses repeated time points with paired structure

Collecting data at two time points is a feature of many study designs, but it does not automatically create paired data. A study could measure one group of users before the update and a completely different group after. The pairing arises from the same individuals appearing in both conditions, not simply from having two collection times.

#### Task Description

**Given:** A hospital administrator finds that patients receiving Treatment X have a higher overall recovery rate than patients receiving Treatment Y, as shown in the chart below. A researcher suspects that Simpson's paradox may be present in this data because the two treatments tend to be prescribed to patients with very different illness severity levels.

If Simpson's paradox is occurring here, what would the researcher expect to find when recovery rates are analyzed separately for each illness severity level?

#### Quiz Options

Treatment X would have a higher recovery rate than Treatment Y in each severity level.


###### Option 2 Correct
Treatment Y would have a higher recovery rate than Treatment X in each severity level.


###### Option 3 Incorrect
Both treatments would show equal recovery rates in each severity level.


###### Option 4 Incorrect

#### Internal Notes

##### Competency
The student analyzes relationships between variables in real-world contexts, including correlation and regression.

##### Evidence Statement
The student interprets correlation.

##### Aligned Content
Simpson's paradox (conceptual)

##### Domain
Healthcare

##### Explanation
Simpson's paradox occurs when a trend observed in aggregated data reverses when the data are separated into meaningful subgroups. Since Treatment X has a higher overall recovery rate, the paradox would mean that within each severity level, Treatment Y actually has the higher recovery rate. The reversal happens because the two treatments are prescribed to patients with very different severity distributions—Treatment X is likely given more often to less severe cases, inflating its overall rate while masking the subgroup-level advantage of Treatment Y.

##### Distractors

###### Option 1
Treatment X higher in each severity level — Confirms the overall trend rather than recognizing the reversal

This option simply extends the overall trend into each subgroup. A student who selects this does not understand that Simpson's paradox requires the direction of the association to reverse when data are disaggregated. If the subgroup trends matched the overall trend, there would be no paradox to identify.

###### Option 3
Equal recovery rates in each severity level — Confuses trend disappearance with trend reversal

This option reflects a misunderstanding that the paradox causes the difference between groups to vanish entirely. While a trend can weaken during disaggregation for various statistical reasons, Simpson's paradox specifically involves a reversal of direction, not the elimination of a difference.

###### Option 4
Mixed results across severity levels — Describes an interaction effect rather than Simpson's paradox

This option describes a crossover interaction in which the relative effectiveness of treatments depends on the severity level. Simpson's paradox requires the trend to reverse consistently across all subgroups, not merely differ from one subgroup to another. A mixed result, where each treatment wins in a different subgroup, would not constitute the paradox.

### Task: wgu_a3ds3_Webpage Load Time Prediction_r8Kn3

#### Task Description

**Given:** A web development team uses the regression equation $\text{Load Time} = 2 + 0.5(\text{Images})$ to predict a webpage's load time (in seconds) based on the number of images on the page.

If the team's performance goal requires the page to load in no more than 8 seconds, what is the maximum number of images that can be included on the page according to this model?



#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
6 images


###### Option 2 Correct
12 images


###### Option 3 Incorrect
16 images


###### Option 4 Incorrect
20 images


#### Quiz Settings

```json
{

  "multipleChoice": false,
  "hasNoneOfTheAbove": false
}
```


#### Internal Notes

##### Competency
The student analyzes relationships between variables in real-world contexts, including correlation and regression.

##### Evidence Statement
The student interprets the slope and intercept of a regression line in context.

##### Aligned Content
Predicted values (informal)

##### Domain
Technology

##### Explanation
To find the maximum number of images, set the predicted load time equal to the 8-second limit and solve for Images. Starting with $8 = 2 + 0.5(\text{Images})$, subtract the intercept from both sides: $8 - 2 = 0.5(\text{Images})$, which gives $6 = 0.5(\text{Images})$. Then divide both sides by the slope: $\text{Images} = 6 \div 0.5 = 12$. According to the model, the page can include a maximum of 12 images and still meet the 8-second load time goal.

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
6 images — Results from correctly subtracting the intercept ($8 - 2 = 6$) but stopping there, failing to divide by the slope. This reflects an incomplete application of the regression equation where the student treats the remaining value as the answer.

###### Option 3
16 images — Results from dividing the target load time directly by the slope ($8 \div 0.5 = 16$) without first accounting for the intercept, showing a misunderstanding of the role the baseline load time plays in the model.

###### Option 4
20 images — Results from adding the intercept to the target load time instead of subtracting it ($(8 + 2) \div 0.5 = 20$), reflecting confusion about the direction of the operation needed when solving the equation for the independent variable.

### Task: wgu_a3ds4_Revenue Residual Interpretation_r7Kn3

#### Task Description

**Given:** A retail chain uses a regression model to predict monthly store revenue (in thousands of dollars) based on advertising spending (in thousands of dollars): $\hat{y} = 3.5x + 12$. During a particular month, one store spent $10{,}000$ dollars on advertising, and the residual for that observation was $-5000$ dollars.

What was the actual monthly revenue for this store, and what does the residual indicate?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Correct
$\$42{,}000$; the model predicted more revenue than the store actually earned


###### Option 2 Incorrect
$\$47{,}000$; the model predicted exactly the store’s revenue


###### Option 3 Incorrect
$\$52{,}000$; the model predicted less revenue than the store actually earned


###### Option 4 Incorrect
$\$40{,}000$; the model predicted less revenue than the store actually earned


#### Quiz Settings

```json
{
  "shuffleOptions": false,
  "multipleChoice": false,
  "hasNoneOfTheAbove": false
}
```


#### Internal Notes

##### Competency
The student analyzes relationships between variables in real-world contexts, including correlation and regression.

##### Evidence Statement
The student interprets the slope and intercept of a regression line in context.

##### Aligned Content
Residual interpretation (conceptual)

##### Domain
Business

##### Explanation
1. **Calculate the predicted value ($\hat{y}$):** Substitute $x = 10$ (since spending is in thousands) into the regression equation: $\hat{y} = 3.5(10) + 12 = 35 + 12 = 47$ thousand dollars.
2. **Determine the actual revenue:** The residual equals the actual value minus the predicted value. Since the residual is $-5$ thousand: $-5 = \text{actual} - 47$, so $\text{actual} = 47 - 5 = 42$ thousand dollars, or $\$42{,}000$.
3. **Interpret the residual:** A negative residual means the actual value fell below the predicted value. Therefore, the model predicted more revenue than the store actually earned (the model overpredicted).

##### Distractors
###### Option 2
This option ignores the residual entirely and treats the predicted revenue, $\$47{,}000$, as the actual revenue. It also incorrectly assumes the model’s prediction matched the store’s actual revenue exactly, even though a nonzero residual means there was prediction error.

###### Option 3
This option comes from mishandling the negative residual by adding $5$ thousand instead of subtracting it, giving $\$52{,}000$. It also reverses the meaning of a negative residual by claiming the model predicted less revenue than the store actually earned.

###### Option 4
This option may result from omitting the intercept and using $3.5(10)=35$ thousand as the prediction, then incorrectly adding $5$ thousand because the residual is negative, giving $\$40{,}000$. It also incorrectly interprets the negative residual as meaning the model underpredicted rather than overpredicted.

##### Explanation

Scatterplot A shows the strongest positive linear trend, Scatterplot B shows a weak negative linear trend, and Scatterplot C shows little to no linear trend. import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
```python
import numpy as np

def setup_style():
    """Configure matplotlib style to match scientific plotting standards."""
    plt.rcParams['font.family'] = 'DejaVu Sans'
    plt.rcParams['font.size'] = 10
    plt.rcParams['axes.titlesize'] = 14
    plt.rcParams['axes.titleweight'] = 'bold'
    plt.rcParams['axes.labelsize'] = 12
    plt.rcParams['axes.labelcolor'] = '#333333'
    plt.rcParams['xtick.labelsize'] = 10
    plt.rcParams['ytick.labelsize'] = 10
    plt.rcParams['xtick.color'] = '#666666'
    plt.rcParams['ytick.color'] = '#666666'
    plt.rcParams['axes.edgecolor'] = '#333333'
    plt.rcParams['axes.linewidth'] = 1.0
    plt.rcParams['grid.color'] = '#CCCCCC'
    plt.rcParams['grid.linewidth'] = 0.5
    plt.rcParams['figure.facecolor'] = 'white'
    plt.rcParams['axes.facecolor'] = 'white'

def plot_image():
    """Generate the scientific visualization."""
    setup_style()

    # Use a wide figure layout to accommodate three side-by-side square plots
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 5))

    # Configuration for the three plots
    # Plot A: Strong positive (r=0.90)
    # Plot B: Weak negative (r=-0.30)
    # Plot C: No correlation (r=0.05)
    plots_config = [
        {'title': 'Plot A', 'r': 0.90, 'n': 80},
        {'title': 'Plot B', 'r': -0.30, 'n': 80},
        {'title': 'Plot C', 'r': 0.05, 'n': 80}
    ]

    np.random.seed(42)  # Ensure reproducibility

    for ax, config in zip(axes, plots_config):
        # Generate correlated data
        # Mean centered at 50, 50; std deviation approx 15 to fill 0-100 range
        mean = [50, 50]
        std_dev = 15
        cov = [
            [std_dev**2, config['r'] * std_dev**2], 
            [config['r'] * std_dev**2, std_dev**2]
        ]
        
        data = np.random.multivariate_normal(mean, cov, config['n'])
        
        # Clip data to keeping it strictly within axes limits
        x = np.clip(data[:, 0], 2, 98)
        y = np.clip(data[:, 1], 2, 98)

        # Plot scatter points
        # Blue fill (#1f77b4) with dark edge for distinctness
        ax.scatter(x, y, 
                   color='#1f77b4', 
                   edgecolor='#333333', 
                   linewidth=0.8, 
                   s=60, 
                   alpha=1.0, 
                   zorder=3)

        # Labels and Title
        ax.set_title(config['title'], pad=12)
        ax.set_xlabel('KPI 1 Value')
        ax.set_ylabel('KPI 2 Value')

        # Axes limits
        ax.set_xlim(0, 100)
        ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
        
        # Grid behind data
        ax.grid(True, linestyle='-', zorder=0)
        ax.set_axisbelow(True)

        # Spines: Hide top and right
        ax.spines['top'].set_visible(False)
        ax.spines['right'].set_visible(False)
        
        # Ticks: Outward facing
        ax.tick_params(direction='out', length=4)
        
        # Force square aspect ratio for the plot area
        ax.set_aspect('equal', adjustable='box')

    plt.tight_layout()
    return fig

if __name__ == "__main__":
    fig = plot_image()
    plt.savefig('plot.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    print("Image saved as plot.png")
```

Comparing direction, form, strength

Education

##### Explanation
