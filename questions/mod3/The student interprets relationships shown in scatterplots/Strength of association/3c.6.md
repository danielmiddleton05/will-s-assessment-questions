#### Task Description

A school counselor collects data on the number of absences and semester grade point average (GPA) for 30 students. The results are displayed in the scatterplot below.

![A scatterplot with number of absences on the x-axis and semester GPA on the y-axis. The data points show a general downward trend from left to right, but the points are moderately spread out around the trend, with several points notably above or below the general pattern.](https://k3-production-bucket.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/89ba08b3-2981-4b55-8b46-6a28b8d5e429_optimized.jpg)

The counselor states: "These data show that knowing how many absences a student has allows us to predict their GPA with near-perfect accuracy."

How well does the scatterplot support the counselor's claim about prediction accuracy?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
The scatterplot fully supports this statement because the data points are tightly clustered along a line, leaving little room for prediction error.


###### Option 2 Incorrect
The scatterplot does not support this statement because, while a few data points may appear to follow a pattern, no consistent trend exists in the overall data.


###### Option 3 Correct
The scatterplot partially supports this statement because, while a downward trend is visible, the spread of data points limits prediction accuracy.


###### Option 4 Incorrect
The scatterplot fully supports this statement because collecting data from 30 students is a large enough sample to eliminate any variability in the relationship.

##### Explanation
The scatterplot shows a moderate negative association (r = –0.58)—a general downward trend exists, but the data points are spread out around that trend rather than tightly clustered. This means there is some predictive value in knowing a student's absences, but the spread indicates that predictions would come with considerable uncertainty. The counselor's claim of "near-perfect accuracy" overstates the degree of certainty; the data only partially support the statement. Recognizing the moderate correlation and spread as limiting prediction certainty is the key to correctly interpreting the strength of the association.

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
This distractor appeals to students who recognize the trend direction but fail to carefully assess the spread of data points. The option claims the data form a "nearly perfect linear pattern," but a correlation of r = –0.58 reflects only a moderate association with substantial spread. Students who conflate the presence of a trend with a strong association may select this option.

###### Option 2
This distractor appeals to students who may expect a perfectly clear or steep trend in order to identify any pattern. The scatterplot does show a visible downward trend (r = –0.58) despite the spread, so claiming there is no pattern misreads the data. This reflects a misconception that moderate associations equate to no association at all.

###### Option 4
This distractor targets the common misconception that a larger number of data points alone determines prediction accuracy. While sample size affects statistical confidence, it does not change the strength of the association between the variables. No sample size can "eliminate variation" in a relationship—the spread of points around the trend determines how certain predictions can be, not the count of observations.
