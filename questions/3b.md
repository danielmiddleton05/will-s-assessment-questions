### Task: wgu_a3bs1_Identifying Dependent Variable_7mN4q

#### Task Description

**Given:** A retail analyst is investigating whether the daily outdoor temperature influences the total sales of hot beverages at a local café. The analyst collects data over 30 days, recording the average daily temperature in degrees Fahrenheit and the total number of hot beverages sold each day.

In this study, which factor is the **response variable**?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
The average daily temperature


###### Option 2 Correct
The total sales of beverages


###### Option 3 Incorrect
The total days of data recorded


###### Option 4 Incorrect
The average price of beverages


#### Quiz Settings

```json
{
  "shuffleOptions": false,
  "multipleChoice": false,
  "hasNoneOfTheAbove": false
}
```


#### Internal Notes

##### Competency
The student analyzes relationships between variables in real-world contexts, including correlation and regression.

##### Evidence Statement
The student identifies independent and dependent variables.

##### Aligned Content
Distinguishing variable roles (C→C, C→Q, Q→Q)

##### Explanation
The response variable (also known as the dependent variable) is the outcome being measured or predicted in a study. In this scenario, the analyst is looking at how sales change as a result of temperature. Therefore, the total sales of beverages is the response variable, while the temperature is the explanatory (independent) variable.

##### Domain
Business

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
The average daily temperature — This is the explanatory or independent variable. It is the factor that is expected to cause a change in the outcome. Students choosing this often reverse the relationship between the cause and the effect.

###### Option 3
The total days of data recorded — This represents the sample size (n = 30). It is a part of the study design and structure, but it is not a variable being measured for its relationship to another factor. Students selecting this may confuse the framework of data collection with the variables within it.

###### Option 4
The average price of beverages — This is a potential confounding or control variable. While price could certainly affect sales, it is not the focus of this specific research question, which is isolated to the impact of temperature. Students may select this if they identify it as a relevant factor in a real-world business context but fail to focus on the specific variables defined in the prompt.

What is the most likely effect on the correlation coefficient ($r$) when this 200th patient is included in the analysis?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Correct
The correlation coefficient will decrease because the outlier does not follow the linear pattern.


###### Option 2 Incorrect
The correlation coefficient will increase because the outlier strengthens the upward trend.


###### Option 3 Incorrect
The correlation coefficient will stay the same because one data point has little effect.


###### Option 4 Incorrect

#### Quiz Settings

```json
{
  "shuffleOptions": false,
  "multipleChoice": false,
  "hasNoneOfTheAbove": false
}
```


#### Internal Notes

##### Competency
The student analyzes relationships between variables in real-world contexts, including correlation and regression.

##### Evidence Statement
The student interprets correlation

##### Aligned Content
Effects of outliers on correlation

##### Explanation
The correlation coefficient ($r$) measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship. When a dataset has a near-perfect correlation ($r = 0.999$), the points lie almost exactly on a straight line. An outlier that is "off-trend"—meaning it does not follow the linear pattern established by the rest of the data—will pull the correlation coefficient closer to zero. Even in a sample of 200, a distant outlier increases the sum of squared deviations from the regression line, thereby weakening the $r$ value.

##### Domain
Health

##### Distractors
###### Option 2
This reflects the misconception that higher y-values or "more data" in a positive trend automatically increase the correlation. However, $r$ measures the consistency of the linear pattern, not the magnitude of the values.

###### Option 3
This is incorrect because $r$ is highly sensitive to outliers. While a larger sample size ($n=200$) provides some stability, a "distant" outlier (especially one significantly far from the trend line) will still mathematically lower the correlation coefficient.

###### Option 4
This option focuses on the classification of the resulting correlation rather than the *effect* of the outlier. While the $r$ value may remain numerically high, the addition of an off-trend point necessarily decreases the strength of the relationship compared to the original 0.999.

Since Treatment X has a higher overall recovery rate, the paradox would mean that within each severity level, Treatment Y actually has the higher recovery rate. The reversal happens because the two treatments are prescribed to patients with very different severity distributions—Treatment X is likely given more often to less severe cases, inflating its overall rate while masking the subgroup-level advantage of Treatment Y.

##### Distractors

###### Option 1
Treatment X higher in each severity level — Confirms the overall trend rather than recognizing the reversal

This option simply extends the overall trend into each subgroup. A student who selects this does not understand that Simpson's paradox requires the direction of the association to reverse when data are disaggregated. If the subgroup trends matched the overall trend, there would be no paradox to identify.

###### Option 3
Equal recovery rates in each severity level — Confuses trend disappearance with trend reversal

This option reflects a misunderstanding that the paradox causes the difference between groups to vanish entirely. While a trend can weaken during disaggregation for various statistical reasons, Simpson's paradox specifically involves a reversal of direction, not the elimination of a difference.

###### Option 4
Mixed results across severity levels — Describes an interaction effect rather than Simpson's paradox

This option describes a crossover interaction in which the relative effectiveness of treatments depends on the severity level. Simpson's paradox requires the trend to reverse consistently across all subgroups, not merely differ from one subgroup to another. A mixed result, where each treatment wins in a different subgroup, would not constitute the paradox.

##### Explanation
To find the maximum number of images, set the predicted load time equal to the 8-second limit and solve for Images. ###### Option 4
