#### Task Description

A hospital is evaluating how often patients experience a mild side effect from a common medication. Large-scale clinical trials established a theoretical side-effect rate of 10%. Three hospital departments independently tracked their patients' experiences with this medication:

- Department A treated 30 patients: 4 experienced the side effect (13.3%)
- Department B treated 150 patients: 16 experienced the side effect (10.7%)
- Department C treated 800 patients: 79 experienced the side effect (9.9%)

What does the comparison of these three departments' results to the theoretical rate reveal about the effect of sample size on observed side-effect rates?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
Smaller patient groups produce more accurate observed rates because they are more sensitive to detecting elevated side-effect levels than larger groups.


###### Option 2 Incorrect
Sample size does not meaningfully affect observed rates; the departmental differences reflect patient population variability rather than the number of patients tracked.


###### Option 3 Correct
Larger patient groups tend to produce observed side-effect rates that fall closer to the theoretical rate established in clinical trials.


###### Option 4 Incorrect
Larger patient groups systematically undercount side effects, causing their observed rates to consistently fall below the theoretical rate established in trials.

##### Explanation
As sample sizes increase, empirical (observed) frequencies tend to converge toward the theoretical probability—a principle related to the law of large numbers. Department A's small sample of 30 patients produced the largest deviation from the theoretical 10% rate, while Department C's much larger sample of 800 patients yielded a result very close to 10%. This pattern illustrates that random variation has a greater visible effect in smaller samples, and that larger samples provide more stable and representative empirical estimates. None of the departments' results necessarily indicate an error, an unreliable theory, or a change in the true rate; they simply reflect the natural behavior of probability across different sample sizes.

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
Incorrectly interprets Department A's higher observed rate as evidence that smaller samples are more sensitive or accurate. This targets students who confuse random fluctuation in small samples with meaningful signal, rather than recognizing that the larger deviation from the theoretical rate is a predictable consequence of having fewer observations.

###### Option 2
Incorrectly dismisses the role of sample size entirely and attributes the differences to external factors. This targets students who do not recognize the relationship between sample size and the stability of empirical estimates, instead assuming that any variation must be explained by differences in the groups themselves rather than by chance.

###### Option 4
Incorrectly treats Department C's result of 9.9%—which is slightly below 10%—as evidence of a systematic downward bias in larger samples. This targets students who confuse the direction of a single sample's random deviation with a predictable pattern, rather than understanding that a large sample's result falling just below the theoretical rate is consistent with normal random variation around that rate.
