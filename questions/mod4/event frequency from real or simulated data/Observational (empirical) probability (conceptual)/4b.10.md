#### Task Description

**Given:** A hospital analyzed patient records and found that 180 out of 600 patients admitted over six months were readmitted within 30 days.

Why is the proportion $\frac{180}{600}$ considered an estimate rather than an exact probability?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
The proportion is calculated using a finite population instead of an infinite number of trials.


###### Option 2 Incorrect
The sample size of 600 is too small to produce a result with 100% mathematical certainty.


###### Option 3 Correct
The data represents a specific sample that could vary with different observations.


###### Option 4 Incorrect
The calculation assumes that the likelihood of readmission is the same for every patient.

##### Explanation
Empirical probability is always an estimate because it depends on the specific sample observed. A different six-month period or different hospital might yield different proportions. The true underlying probability can only be approximated through observation, making any single calculation an estimate based on sample data rather than a known theoretical value.

##### Domain
Health

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
Confuses the frequentist definition of probability (limit of relative frequency over infinite trials) with the reason why a specific calculation is an estimate. While empirical probability approaches the true probability as trials increase, being "finite" is a characteristic of the data collection, not the fundamental reason the resulting proportion is treated as an estimate in statistics.

###### Option 2
Suggests that "certainty" is a function of sample size. Students may select this if they believe that a larger sample size would eventually lead to an "exact" probability, failing to realize that any empirical measure—no matter how large the sample—remains an estimate of the underlying population parameter.

###### Option 4
Focuses on the assumption of homogeneity (independence and identical distribution). While this is a common assumption in basic probability models, it does not address the relationship between sample data and the estimation of probability; an estimate remains an estimate even if the underlying probabilities for individuals differ.
