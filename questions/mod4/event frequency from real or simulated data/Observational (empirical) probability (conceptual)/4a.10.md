#### Task Description

A hospital reviewed 600 patient admissions from the past six months and found that 180 resulted in readmission within 30 days. The hospital wants to use this data to estimate the probability that a future patient admitted under similar conditions will be readmitted within 30 days.

Why is the proportion $\frac{180}{600}$ considered an estimate rather than an exact probability?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
The proportion is calculated using a finite population instead of an infinite number of trials.


###### Option 2 Incorrect
The sample size of 600 is too small to produce a result with 100% mathematical certainty.


###### Option 3 Correct
The proportion is based on observed data from a specific sample, and a different group of patients could produce a different result.


###### Option 4 Incorrect
The proportion would only be exact if every future patient had the same inherent likelihood of readmission.

##### Explanation
Empirical probability is always an estimate because it depends on the specific sample observed. A different six-month period or different hospital might yield different proportions. The true underlying probability can only be approximated through observation, making any single calculation an estimate based on sample data rather than a known theoretical value.

##### Domain

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
Confuses the frequentist definition of probability (limit of relative frequency over infinite trials) with the reason why a specific calculation is an estimate. While empirical probability approaches the true probability as trials increase, being "finite" is a characteristic of the data collection, not the fundamental reason the resulting proportion is treated as an estimate in statistics.

###### Option 2
Suggests that "certainty" is a function of sample size. Students may select this if they believe that a larger sample size would eventually lead to an "exact" probability, failing to realize that any empirical measure remains an estimate of the underlying probability regardless of sample size.

###### Option 4
Focuses on the assumption of homogeneity (independence and identical distribution). While this is a common assumption in basic probability models, it does not address the relationship between sample data and the estimation of probability; an estimate remains an estimate even if the underlying probabilities for individuals vary.
