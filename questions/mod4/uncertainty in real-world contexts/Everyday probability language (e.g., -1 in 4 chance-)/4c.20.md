#### Task Description

**Given:** A public health report states that "there is a 1 in 10 chance of experiencing mild side effects from a particular vaccine." A clinic administered this vaccine to 200 patients.

Based on the report, what is a reasonable expectation about side effects among these 200 patients?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
If 9 consecutive patients have no side effects, the next patient is more likely to experience them.


###### Option 2 Incorrect
Approximately 2 patients are likely to experience side effects, since "1 in 10" translates to a 1% probability.


###### Option 3 Correct
Approximately 20 patients should experience side effects, though the actual number may vary.


###### Option 4 Incorrect
It is almost impossible for more than 20 patients to experience side effects, since the probability limits the rate to 10%.

##### Explanation
"1 in 10" is everyday language expressing a probability of $\frac{1}{10} = 0.10 = 10\%$. Applying this to 200 patients, we would expect approximately $200 \times 0.10 = 20$ patients to experience mild side effects. However, because probability describes approximate long-run expectations rather than exact guarantees, the actual number may vary around 20. The correct answer reflects both the proper interpretation of the probability language and the inherent uncertainty of real-world outcomes.

##### Distractors

###### Option 1
If 9 consecutive patients have no side effects, the next patient is more likely to experience them — Gambler's Fallacy

This targets the misconception that previous outcomes influence future independent events. Students may believe that after a streak of patients without side effects, the next patient's risk increases to "compensate," rather than understanding that each patient independently faces a 10% chance regardless of what happened to prior patients.

###### Option 2
Approximately 2 patients are likely to experience side effects, since "1 in 10" translates to a 1% probability — Incorrectly converts the ratio

This targets students who misinterpret "1 in 10" as equivalent to 1%, confusing it with "1 in 100," rather than correctly recognizing that $\frac{1}{10} = 10\%$. This error yields $200 \times 0.01 = 2$ patients instead of the correct calculation of $200 \times 0.10 = 20$ patients.

###### Option 4
It is almost impossible for more than 20 patients to experience side effects, since the probability limits the rate to 10% — Treats probability as a hard upper limit

This targets the misconception that a probability acts as a ceiling on outcomes rather than describing an average expectation. While 20 is the expected value, probability inherently involves variability, and the actual number of patients experiencing side effects can plausibly fall above or below 20 in any given group. A 10% probability does not cap the number at exactly 10% of the sample.
