#### Task Description

**Given:** A data center operates 200 solid-state drives (SSDs) with a manufacturer-reported 1.5% annual failure rate. After one year, 6 of the 200 drives failed rather than the approximately 3 that were expected.

Why is observing 6 failed drives still consistent with the manufacturer's 1.5% annual failure rate?

#### Quiz Options

###### Option 1 Incorrect
Because the expected value represents only a long-run average, not a single year's outcome, so the 6 observed failures cannot meaningfully be compared to the expected 3.


###### Option 2 Incorrect
The difference between 3 and 6 is small relative to 200 total drives, so any result that keeps the overall failure rate under 5% would match the manufacturer's claim.


###### Option 3 Correct
Probability predicts likely outcomes but does not guarantee them, so random chance can cause actual results to differ from the expected value.


###### Option 4 Incorrect
The manufacturer's 1.5% rate is only an estimate, so the observation of 6 failures most likely means the true rate is about 3%, making 6 the actual expected number.

##### Explanation
A 1.5% annual failure rate applied to 200 drives yields an expected value of approximately 3 failures (0.015 × 200 = 3). However, probability describes likelihood, not a guaranteed outcome. Random variation inherent in any probabilistic process means the actual number of failures in a given year can be higher or lower than the expected value. Observing 6 failures does not invalidate the 1.5% rate; it reflects the natural variability that surrounds any probabilistic prediction. The student must recognize that deviation from an expected value is a normal feature of uncertainty, not evidence that the probability itself is wrong or inapplicable.

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
Because the expected value represents only a long-run average, not a single year's outcome, so the 6 observed failures cannot meaningfully be compared to the expected 3. — Incorrectly claims that expected value has no relevance to any single period, rendering the comparison itself invalid. This targets students who confuse the law of large numbers with the applicability of expected value, believing that a single year's observation cannot be evaluated against a probabilistic prediction. In reality, the expected value is a valid benchmark for any single period; the reason 6 is consistent is that random variation naturally produces deviations, not that expected value is inapplicable to one year.

###### Option 2
The difference between 3 and 6 is small relative to 200 total drives, so any result that keeps the overall failure rate under 5% would match the manufacturer's claim — Introduces an arbitrary threshold to judge consistency rather than understanding probability distributions. This targets students who reason that as long as failures stay "low" relative to the total, the result matches the rate. In reality, consistency is explained by random variation around the expected value, not by falling below a made-up cutoff.

###### Option 4
The manufacturer's 1.5% rate is only an estimate, so the observation of 6 failures most likely means the true rate is about 3%, making 6 the actual expected number — Concludes from a single year's data that the manufacturer's stated rate must be wrong rather than recognizing normal random variation. This targets students who assume the gap between predicted and observed results must mean the original rate is inaccurate, rather than recognizing that probability-based predictions naturally produce a range of outcomes even when the underlying rate is correct.
