##### Explanation
To find the percent decrease from March to April, first read the revenue values from the line graph: March is $70,000 and April is $50,000. The decrease in revenue is $70,000 − $50,000 = $20,000. The percent decrease is calculated as (decrease ÷ original value) × 100 = ($20,000 ÷ $70,000) × 100 ≈ 28.6%. This requires both accurately reading values from the graph and applying the percent change formula using the original (March) value as the denominator.

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
A candidate might arrive at 22.2% by incorrectly using the sum of the two months' revenues as the denominator rather than the original month alone—computing $20,000 ÷ ($70,000 + $50,000) × 100 ≈ 16.7% or a similar erroneous base—or by misreading one of the values from the graph (e.g., reading March as $65,000 instead of $70,000 and then computing $15,000 ÷ $65,000 ≈ 23.1%, which rounds in the neighborhood of 22.2%). This reflects a misunderstanding of which value serves as the base in a percent decrease calculation.

###### Option 2
A candidate might select 25.7% by mistakenly using a value between the correct base and an incorrect base, such as averaging the March and April values to get $60,000 and computing $20,000 ÷ $60,000 × 100 ≈ 33.3%, or by slight misreading of graph values. This option is close enough to the correct answer to attract candidates who set up the calculation correctly but make a minor arithmetic or reading error.

###### Option 4
A candidate might arrive at 40.0% by using the April value ($50,000) as the denominator instead of the March value ($70,000), computing $20,000 ÷ $50,000 × 100 = 40.0%. This is a common error in percent change problems where a student divides by the new value rather than the original value, reflecting a misunderstanding of the percent decrease formula.

##### Explanation
The current graph's y-axis runs from 4.0 to 5.0, which means the 0.6-day difference between Hospital A (4.8 days) and Hospital B (4.2 days) fills most of the visible axis range. This makes Hospital A's bar appear dramatically taller than Hospital B's. If the y-axis were rescaled to start at 0 days, both bars would extend to roughly similar heights (4.8 and 4.2 out of a range ending around 5), and the 0.6-day difference would be visually proportional to the full bar lengths—appearing as a modest gap rather than a dramatic one.

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
The bars would appear to be exactly the same height because the 0.6-day difference is only visible when the y-axis is truncated to a narrow range — Targets students who recognize that the current scale is misleading but mistakenly believe that the difference between the values is entirely dependent on the truncated scale. While the difference becomes less exaggerated when starting at zero, a 0.6-day gap is still a distinct, measurable value that remains visible on a 0-to-5 scale.

###### Option 3
The bars would appear even more different because extending the y-axis range amplifies gaps between values — Targets students who confuse a longer axis with greater visual separation. In reality, extending the y-axis to start at zero compresses the data into a smaller portion of the graph, which shrinks the visual gap rather than enlarging it.

###### Option 4
The bars would appear the same as they do now because the underlying data values do not change — Targets students who correctly note that the data remains identical but fail to distinguish between data values and their visual representation. Changing the axis range does not alter the numbers, but it fundamentally changes how the difference between those numbers looks to a viewer.

**Given:** A quality assurance team tested the download speeds (in Mbps) of two wireless routers under identical network conditions across five trials:

- Router A: $48, 50, 52, 49, 51$
- Router B: $30, 70, 45, 60, 45$

Both routers averaged 50 Mbps.

Based on these results, how do the download speeds of the two routers compare in terms of variability?

###### Option 2 Correct
Router B shows greater variability because its trial results are spread far from the average.


###### Option 3 Incorrect
Both routers show equal variability because they share the same average speed.


###### Option 4 Incorrect
Router B shows lower variability because its wide range of results balances around the average.

#### Internal Notes

##### Competency
The student interprets real-world data using graphical displays and descriptive statistics.

##### Evidence Statement
The student calculates measures of center and spread

##### Aligned Content
Standard deviation (conceptual)

##### Domain
Technology

##### Explanation
Standard deviation measures how spread out data values are from the mean. Although both routers share the same average of 50 Mbps, their individual trial results differ dramatically in spread. Router A's values (48–52) cluster tightly around the mean, deviating by only 1–2 Mbps per trial. Router B's values (30–70) are scattered far from the mean, with individual deviations as large as 20 Mbps. This means Router B has a much larger standard deviation—and therefore greater variability—than Router A. A shared average does not imply shared consistency.

##### Distractors

###### Option 1
Router A shows greater variability because no two trial results are exactly the same — Confuses uniqueness of data points with spread. The fact that all five values in Router A are distinct does not mean they are widely spread. All of Router A's values fall within 4 Mbps of the mean, indicating very low variability. Meanwhile, Router B has a repeated value (45) yet its values range 40 Mbps from the mean, demonstrating much greater variability.

###### Option 3
Both routers show equal variability because they share the same average speed — Confuses the mean (a measure of center) with standard deviation (a measure of spread). Two datasets can have identical means yet very different levels of variability. The mean tells us nothing about how spread out the individual values are.

###### Option 4
Router B shows lower variability because its wide range of results balances around the average — Incorrectly reasons that values spreading symmetrically around the mean creates consistency. A wider spread of data points around the average actually produces a larger standard deviation, indicating more variability, not less—regardless of whether the values appear to balance out.

##### Explanation
Because each clinic surveyed a different number of patients, raw counts of "Excellent" ratings cannot be compared directly. Instead, the percentage of "Excellent" ratings must be calculated relative to each clinic's total. Clinic A: $60/240 = 25\%$, Clinic B: $68/200 = 34\%$, Clinic C: $45/100 = 45\%$, Clinic D: $40/120 \approx 33.3\%$. Clinic C has the highest percentage of "Excellent" ratings at 45%, even though it has fewer total "Excellent" responses than Clinics A or B.

##### Distractors
###### Option 1
Clinic A has the largest total number of surveyed patients (240) and the second-highest raw count of "Excellent" ratings (60). Students may select Clinic A if they assume the largest clinic must have the highest satisfaction rate, or if they confuse overall clinic size with the proportion of top-rated experiences. However, Clinic A's "Excellent" percentage is only 25%, the lowest of all four clinics.

###### Option 2
Clinic B has the highest raw count of "Excellent" ratings (68). Students who compare raw numbers across columns without dividing by each clinic's total would identify Clinic B as the answer. This is the most common error—mistaking the largest count for the largest rate when group sizes differ. Clinic B's actual "Excellent" percentage is 34%, below Clinic C's 45%.

###### Option 4
Clinic D's "Excellent" percentage is approximately 33.3%, which is close to Clinic B's 34%. Students who attempt the percentage calculations but make arithmetic errors, transpose values, or round imprecisely may arrive at Clinic D. Additionally, students who divide 40 by the "Excellent" row total (213) rather than by the Clinic D column total (120) would get approximately 18.8%, potentially leading to further confusion during comparison.
